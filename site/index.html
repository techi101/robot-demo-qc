A data-quality pass on teleoperated robot demonstrations

Checking robot demos before they reach training

I ran 149 human-teleoperated ALOHA episodes (104,400 frames from three public LeRobot datasets) through a QC pipeline that decides what goes into a training release. The hard checks found nothing broken. The soft checks found two coffee episodes where the operator held the left forearm 0.8 to 0.9 rad away from the typical demo, a steady 80 ms gap between command and robot, and schema drift that would break a naive merge. Then I corrupted copies of the data on purpose, to show which checks catch what and where they go blind.

What gets checked

Every episode goes through nine checks. A fail drops the episode from the release. A warn keeps it but writes the reason into the manifest, because an unusual demo is often a valid demo and deleting it silently would bias the dataset.

CheckCatchesRuleLevel

Dataset-level checks run once per dataset: episode metadata vs parquet rows, index spans, video segment duration vs frame count for all four cameras, and stats.json vs statistics recomputed from the raw frames.

Findings on real data

Data: lerobot/aloha_static_coffee (MIT), aloha_static_battery (Apache-2.0) and aloha_static_cups_open (MIT), pinned to exact Hub revisions. Only the parquet files were downloaded (5.6 MB); videos were not.

  1. The hard checks found nothing, and that is a real result. Every one of 104,400 frames is exactly 20.0 ms after the previous one, frame indices are contiguous, there are no NaN or inf values, every episode's metadata matches its parquet rows, and every video segment's duration equals frames / fps to 0.0 ms on all four cameras. The control run below fires zero fail-level checks on these episodes.
  2. Two coffee episodes were done a different way. In episodes 0 and 22 the left forearm roll averages −0.27 and −0.37 rad, against a median of +0.56 over the other 48 (robust z −7.9 and −8.9). Episode 22's wrist rotate is also shifted the other way (z +6.7), which looks like the operator rolled the forearm one way and counter-rotated the wrist. The pipeline keeps both and flags them: a policy trained on 48 demos of one grasp and 2 of another may average the two into neither.
  3. The robot trails the operator by 80 ms, every time. State lags action by 4 frames in all 50 coffee episodes, and by 4 or 5 frames in battery (44 / 5) and cups (30 / 20). That is stable enough to be a property of the hardware, which makes it a good sync baseline: the fault test shows a 10-frame shift is caught every time.
  4. The effort channel carries less signal than its shape suggests. Coffee is the only one of the three with observation.effort. Its values are quantized in steps of 2.69, and the forearm-roll effort reads exactly zero in 57% (left) and 49% (right) of frames. Anyone training a force-aware model on this should know two of 14 channels are mostly a constant.
  5. The three datasets do not share a schema. Battery reports robot_type: "unknown" and names its joints motor_0 … motor_13; the other two use named ALOHA joints. Only coffee has effort. Every episode in a dataset has the same length (1100, 600 and 400 frames), so episode length cannot reveal a cut-off demo here. The gripper action and gripper state also live in different ranges, so value comparisons skip the two gripper joints.

Proving the checks work

Clean data shows that the checks stay quiet. It does not show that they would catch anything. So for every episode that passed, I made corrupted copies, one fault at a time, and re-ran the checks against the clean reference. That is corrupted episodes plus an untouched control set.

Where the checks go blind

A frozen sensor on a joint that is not moving looks exactly like a joint that is not moving. Freezing a random joint for 1.5 s is caught 40% of the time overall, but most misses changed the signal by almost nothing. Counting only freezes that moved the reading by more than 0.05 rad, recall is 80% (59 of 74). The rest are slow drifts where the commanded motion during the freeze stayed under the threshold.

A 0.08 rad one-frame spike is the size of real fast motion (the largest real per-frame action step is 0.10 rad). It is caught 47% of the time. A 0.30 rad spike is caught every time. Catching smaller glitches needs a model of expected motion, not a step threshold.

Release manifest

Each run writes a manifest that pins exactly what went into a release: the source revision, the hash of the QC thresholds, the hash of the QC code, and the reason behind every dropped or flagged episode. Change one threshold and the release id changes with it.

Decisions and unknowns

  
Most thresholds had to be set without ground truth labels. Here is what I chose, why, and what I could not check.

  

    
Timing gap at 1.5 frame periods
Real data has zero jitter (every step exactly 20 ms), so anything past 30 ms is a lost frame. On hardware with jitter I would set this from the measured dt distribution instead.

    
Frozen: 0.10 rad over 0.5 s
Set from the sweep above. The risk is that it was tuned on the same 149 episodes it is reported on; a new robot or task needs a fresh sweep before the number is trusted.

    
Spike: 4 × p99.9 step, floor 0.05 rad
The reference p99.9 is the median across episodes, so a few corrupted episodes cannot raise their own threshold. The largest real step is at most about 2 × p99.9 on any joint, leaving room before 4×.

    
Outliers warn, never fail
Robust z above 6 on any feature. With 31 to 45 features per episode some false flags are expected, and an odd demo may be the most useful one. A human or a video check should decide, not a z-score.

    
Grippers skipped in value checks
Leader and follower grippers report in different ranges, so comparing action to state there would be noise. Frozen-sensor and spike checks still cover them.

    
What I could not verify
Whether each demo actually completed the task (needs video or a success label); why episodes 0 and 22 differ; whether effort is in mA or another unit (the 2.69 step suggests a raw current reading); and what 4 vs 5 frames of lag means for the controller. Effort-specific checks ran only on coffee, the one dataset that has it.

  






  
Run it

  
git clone https://github.com/techi101/robot-demo-qc
cd robot-demo-qc
pip install -r requirements.txt
python scripts/download.py     # ~5.6 MB of parquet, pinned revisions
python -m qc.run               # report, manifest, site data
python -m pytest -q            # 15 tests on synthetic episodes

  
The whole run, including  fault-injected episodes, takes well under two minutes on a laptop.
